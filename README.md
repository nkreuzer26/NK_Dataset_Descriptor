# NK_Dataset_Descriptor

These data represent the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita for Argentina, Chile, and Brazil from 1950 to 2000. GDP measures the total value of all official goods and services produced within a country in a given year. When expressed per capita, GDP represents the average level of economic output, and by proxy, material living standards, per person. 

The data were taken from the Maddison Project Database, a historical dataset aiming to reconstruct and standardize national income estimates across countries hosted by the University of Groningen1. For the years of interest, 1950-2000, GDP per capita is based on internationally comparable purchasing power parity (PPP) benchmarks (1). PPP adjustments correct for cross-country cost-of-living differences, ensuring that one unit of currency has the same purchasing power across countries. Values are expressed in 2011 international dollars, meaning that inflation has been removed and prices are held constant at 2011 levels, allowing for comparisons across countries and time. Historical GDP levels are then extrapolated backward and forward from the benchmark years using each country’s real GDP per capita growth rates (1). This method preserves country-specific economic trajectories while still expressing all values in an internationally consistent manner, which is especially important for Latin America because exchange rates and inflation have varied substantially during the last 50 years (2). 

The purpose of this data is to examine the relationship between U.S. political and military interventions in Argentina, Brazil, and Chile and their subsequent effects on the average quality of life in these countries. While GDP is well-suited for identifying broad macroeconomic trends across countries and is one of the most consistently measured indicators of average material output over time, it is by no means a perfect metric. For example, by design, GDP represents the perspective of national-level economic aggregates instead of individuals or households (3). As a result, it excludes large portions of economic activity that are not formally recorded in markets, such as unpaid domestic labor, informal work, and care labor (1,3). Furthermore, underground and illicit economic activity, including informal trade, unregistered labor, and drug-related economies, constitutes a significant share of economic life in parts of Argentina, Brazil, and Chile but are largely absent from official GDP statistics (3,4). 

In addition, the dataset does not capture how economic output is distributed within countries; periods of rising GDP per capita may coincide with stagnant or declining living standards for large segments of the population, particularly in countries with high income inequality (3). 

GDP also leaves much to be desired as a proxy for quality of life. It measures market production but excludes social and environmental costs associated with economic growth, including pollution, ecological degradation, and resource depletion (1,3). Improvements in product quality or access to information are often undercounted, while increased commercialization of everyday activities can artificially inflate growth (1,3). Moreover, GDP does not capture nonmaterial dimensions of well-being like health, education, political stability, personal security, or social cohesion3. For these reasons, GDP per capita should be interpreted as an indicator of economic capacity rather than overall well-being, and other metrics to represent quality of life should be explored.
